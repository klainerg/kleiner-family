The Deportation Route and the Ghetto

In the summer of 1941, after Sokyriany returned to Romanian-German control, the rounding up and deportation of the Jews of the town and its surroundings began. Among those deported were members of both the Kleiner and Beznos families.

According to the historical documentation on the Jews of Sokyriany, one of the first major stops on the route was Edineț. Jews from Sokyriany and other settlements in northern Bessarabia were gathered there in harsh conditions. A source describing the fate of Sokyriany's Jews recounts that they stayed in Edineț for about four weeks before being sent on their way again.

From Edineț, a long march eastward toward the Dniester River began. According to the same source, the deportees from Sokyriany marched for 32 days to Yampol. There was almost no food along the way; the deportees survived in part on grass and rotten beets, and many died or were murdered on the journey. At that time, Yampol was one of the main crossing points through which Jews from Bessarabia and Bukovina were transferred into Romanian-controlled territory east of the Dniester.

At this stage, according to family memories, the fates of the two families diverged.

The Kleiner family — Haim, Perl, Seindle and Yosef — did not reach the ghetto. According to the memories passed down in the family, they were murdered on the way during the deportation. The sources on the route from Sokyriany fit this memory well: they describe a long and murderous journey on which many of the deportees never reached their destination.

The Beznos family, by contrast, survived the journey and eventually reached Kopaygorod (Копайгород / Kopaygorod) in the Vinnytsia region.

In the autumn of 1941, tens of thousands of Jews from Bessarabia and Bukovina were transferred through the crossings at Yampol and Mohyliv-Podilskyi into the Vinnytsia region. Kopaygorod was one of the places the deportees reached: by the end of 1941, about 6,000 Jewish deportees from Bessarabia and Bukovina had arrived there.

The probable route of the Beznos family was therefore:

Sokyriany → Edineț → the deportation march eastward → crossing the Dniester near Yampol or Mohyliv-Podilskyi → the Vinnytsia region → Kopaygorod.

In Kopaygorod, thousands of Jews were initially held in a camp area near Kopay station, and were subsequently moved into the town. The sources describe thousands of Jews from Bessarabia and Bukovina arriving there as early as October 1941, and the camp's Jews being returned to Kopaygorod at the end of November. Later, an organized ghetto existed there under Romanian administration.

Although Kopaygorod is in Ukraine's Vinnytsia region, in 1941–1944 it lay within the territory the Romanians called Transnistria. Therefore, the memory that the Beznos family was “in a ghetto in the Vinnytsia region” is geographically accurate, even if historical sources identify the place as part of Transnistria.

The family picture that emerges is therefore that both families left Sokyriany in the same wave of deportations or through the same deportation system. Both followed the route of marches and camps eastward from northern Bessarabia. The members of the Kleiner family did not survive the journey and did not reach the ghetto; the members of the Beznos family continued eastward, crossed into the Vinnytsia region and reached the Kopaygorod ghetto.
